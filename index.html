Nobody can answer this question today

A security team ships a patched base image. It clears the scanner, the unit tests pass, and the benchmark shows a small regression that looks acceptable. Then it rolls to a thousand pods and the pager goes off in a service nobody thought was involved.

The gap is not tooling coverage. It is that the three tools you already own each answer a different question, and none of them answers this one.

What being wrong costs

Change-induced failure is not an edge case. It is the majority experience of engineering organisations, and the recovery is slow enough that prediction is worth more than detection.

Latency does not need to become downtime to cost money

Most of a patch's damage never shows up as an outage. Amazon's classic experiment found every 100 ms of added latency cost roughly 1% of sales; Google measured a 400 ms delay reducing search volume by 0.74%, and Bing found 500 ms cut revenue per user by 1.2%. Google and Deloitte's later work put a 0.1-second improvement at around 8% more retail conversions.

Applied to the simulation above: at 90 requests per second, this patch adds 38 ms at checkout. On the Amazon coefficient that is roughly a 0.38% revenue drag — invisible on every dashboard you own, permanent until somebody thinks to look, and entirely predictable beforehand.

The bigger bill is the one you already pay

Because nobody can predict what a change will cost, engineers pad. Resource requests get set for the worst case anyone can imagine, and the padding is never revisited. The result is measurable across tens of thousands of production clusters: average CPU utilisation of 8%, memory at 20%, CPU overprovisioning risen from 40% to 69% in a year. Datadog's own cost research attributes 83% of container spend to idle resources.

Overprovisioning is not carelessness. It is what a team does when it has no model.

This reframes the value. Preventing incidents is a counterfactual and hard to invoice for. Reclaiming headroom is not. A team that can predict what utilisation a change will actually produce can run closer to the line on purpose, and the saving appears on next month's bill.

What we intend to sell

One model, three products, in the order a customer is likely to buy them.

  • Change impact reportsPoint an agent at a cluster, feed it a candidate image, and get a per-service predicted impact map with bounds and an objective-breach list. Sold per prediction or per seat. This is the wedge: no workflow change, no data leaves the cluster, and the output is checkable against the rollout that follows.
  • Rollout gatingThe prediction moves into the deployment pipeline. Waves are ordered by predicted damage — least loaded and least connected clusters first — and a wave is halted when realised impact exceeds the predicted bound. Replaces a canary that watches one service with a model that watches the fleet.
  • Headroom reclamationThe same calibration that predicts damage also says how much padding a service does not need. Priced against measured savings, which makes the return arithmetic rather than argument.

The asset underneath

A library of profiled base images, sidecars and runtimes — the per-code-path cost of each version, measured in our own lab from public images. It compounds with every image profiled, requires no customer data at all, and is what makes the first prediction for a new customer useful on day one rather than after a quarter of learning.

How we get there

The model is validated in simulation. Everything below is the work of showing it survives contact with real infrastructure, and each stage has a condition that decides whether the next one is worth starting.

  1. Break the model on purpose

    Sweep varying cluster sizes, topologies, traffic mixes and utilisation regimes in simulation. Single patch first, then multiple simultaneous patches — which is the honest hard case, because two changes on one service add in service time but compound in waiting time, so patches that are individually safe can be jointly unsafe.

    Proceed when the interaction term is characterised and the model states its own error bars by regime.

  2. Calibrate from real telemetry

    Derive topology, arrival rates, service times and variability from OpenTelemetry and service-mesh data on live clusters. This is where the assumptions that hold in simulation meet merged arrival flows, colocation and autoscaling.

    Proceed when predictions on an unseen cluster land inside the stated bounds at least nine times in ten.

  3. Validate by intervention

    Inject known delays with chaos tooling and check the predicted downstream effect against the measured one. Every injection is a controlled experiment of exactly the kind the model claims to answer, which makes the accuracy record auditable rather than asserted.

    Proceed when injected-delay predictions hold across at least three distinct production architectures.

  4. Collect only what the model needs

    The model consumes moments and a graph, not requests: arrival rates, service times, variability, call multiplicities, code-path usage. All of it computes at the edge. The dependency graph — the genuinely sensitive artefact, since it reveals architecture and organisation — never leaves the customer. The prediction runs where the data already is.

    Proceed when a security review passes with no request payloads and no topology in transit.

  5. Gate the rollout

    Move from report to decision: predicted impact ordering rollout waves, and realised impact checked against predicted bounds at each wave. The product only earns this position once the accuracy record from the previous stages justifies it.

    Proceed when a customer lets the model order a production rollout unattended.

What we want to contribute

Predictive performance engineering is well studied in pieces and barely studied as a whole. Causal analysis explains regressions after the fact. Learned models predict latency without explaining it. Queueing theory explains and bounds but assumes a stability that production does not have. Nothing joins them to answer the question a release engineer actually asks.

  • Forward intervention instead of backward attributionExisting causal work identifies which service caused an incident. We want to predict what a proposed change will do, with bounds, before it happens — the same formalism pointed in the opposite direction.
  • Shared components as the unit of changePublished work models one service's own change. A base image lands on hundreds of services at once, and simultaneous perturbation of many leaves behaves differently from one perturbation repeated.
  • Interaction of concurrent changesReal fleets have several changes in flight. Whether their effects compound, and how strongly, appears to be unstudied and is directly testable.
  • Feedback regimesRetries and timeouts make the propagation graph cyclic and the outcome bimodal. Predicting proximity to that boundary matters more than predicting a mean, and it is where every feedforward model fails.
  • Prediction under privacy constraintsHow accurate can a performance model be when it never sees a request, a service name, or a topology? We think the answer is: nearly as accurate, because the model needs moments rather than data.

Where results are publishable and not commercially load-bearing, we intend to publish them.

Where it is today

The propagation model is validated against discrete-event simulation on request/response fleets: it identifies the affected set exactly, predicts impact within roughly 5% up to 93% utilisation, and proves saturation cases arithmetically rather than statistically. Against a model that ignores queueing it is seven times more accurate at normal load and twenty times more accurate near saturation. The retry-feedback boundary is detected but currently optimistic, so it is reported with a margin.

The same model, unmodified, has since been checked against two harder classes of architecture. On event-driven pipelines it shows that an asynchronous consumer can fall arbitrarily behind while every request-facing latency metric stays flat — the failure mode a latency SLO cannot see by construction. On serverless functions with a concurrency limit it correctly predicts that a patch to a shared dependency raises throttling risk and cost at a function two hops downstream, before that function's own code ever changes, and that headroom which looks wasteful today can be exactly what is absorbing tomorrow's regression. Known blind spots in both extensions — correlated arrivals into a shared resource, a capped function chained behind another capped function — are catalogued and flagged rather than silently mispredicted, and are the current work.
